Apple on August 13, in a letter to the special discovery master overseeing the case, retired Judge José Linares, called the U.S. Department of Justice's (DOJ) motion for reconsideration of a court ruling one that "fails at every level." Linares handles disputes between the parties over document discovery and does not rule on the merits of the antitrust case.

This is the United States, et al. v. Apple Inc. case (No. 2:24-cv-04055). The U.S. Department of Justice and attorneys general from 16 states filed the lawsuit against Apple on March 21, 2024, in the District of New Jersey. The agencies [accuse](https://www.justice.gov/archives/opa/pr/justice-department-sues-apple-monopolizing-smartphone-markets) Apple of illegally maintaining a monopoly in the smartphone market by restricting developers and blocking technologies that reduce users' dependence on the iPhone.

A separate dispute within the case concerns Apple's access to documents from [a range of federal agencies](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/29/doj-fights-apples-bid-for-records-from-14-federal-agencies-in-antitrust-case/) — from NASA to the FBI. The company is seeking their internal assessments of the iPhone's security and competitiveness to support its position in the case. In July, Judge Linares [sided with Apple](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/15/apple-wins-discovery-fight-over-federal-agency-documents-in-doj-antitrust-case/), ruling that all of its requests were relevant to the case.

The DOJ appealed the ruling and attached 13 new agency declarations on the labor costs of searching for documents. Under these declarations, the U.S. State Department estimated the workload at more than 37,000 hours. NASA reported 194,000 email hits, and the Office of Management and Budget reported more than 24 million hits.

Apple, in an [opposition letter](https://9to5mac.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/6/2026/08/gov.uscourts.njd_.544402.493.0.pdf), called reconsideration an "extraordinary remedy" that requires new circumstances or clear judicial error — the DOJ showed neither. The company said the agency declarations were filed late, even though the DOJ had 13 months to prepare them. "The motion fails at every level: its legal arguments are an impermissible re-litigation of already-decided issues; its 'new evidence' was always available and cannot be considered; and even on the merits, the declarations submitted do not establish undue burden," Apple's lawyers wrote in the letter.

According to 9to5Mac, Judge Linares ruled on August 4 that there will be no further exchange of written arguments on the motion. He must now either uphold the ruling in Apple's favor or reconsider it at the DOJ's request.

## Legal pressure and Apple's $100 billion services business

The document dispute is part of broader antitrust pressure on Apple that also affects its services business. In the quarter ended June 27, 2026, revenue from services — App Store, iCloud, subscriptions and other offerings — totaled $30.7 billion. The services segment's gross margin reached 75.6%, [according to the company's report](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0000320193/000032019326000018/a8-kex991q3202606272026.htm). Apple's total quarterly revenue rose 16% year over year to $109.4 billion.

In parallel, in the Epic Games v. Apple case, the company on August 13 proposed cutting the commission on purchases outside the App Store from 27% to 15% for most developers. LEAP News has [previously reported on this proposal](https://leap.uz/en/2026/08/17/apple-appstore-15pct-commission-epic). The Financial Times, in an article headlined "Antitrust attacks start to bite into Apple's $100bn services business," [writes](https://www.ft.com/content/e5533f32-e4c2-4ce1-9d2e-a831e96546b4) about the pressure of antitrust cases on Apple's services business.
