Cybersecurity researchers at Varonis Threat Labs used leading questions to
make Microsoft's Copilot AI assistant reveal how to bypass its own hacking
protections, according to the [company's report dated August 10,
2026](https://www.varonis.com/blog/cosnitch). Varonis updated the report on
[August 18](https://www.varonis.com/blog/cosnitch), the day Microsoft's
patches were released.

## How the AI Gave Away Its Own Secret

Researchers dubbed the technique "meta-hacking." It is a social engineering
method aimed not at a human but at the AI's own "reasoning" mechanism — the
logic Copilot uses to explain its decisions. Researchers asked Copilot why
certain actions were impossible, and each refusal came with a technical
explanation that [revealed details of the system's internal
workings](https://www.varonis.com/blog/cosnitch).

In the industry, this kind of deception is called a jailbreak. Through
specially crafted phrasing, a user forces an AI system to bypass its own
restrictions and provide information or an action its developers
prohibited. In Copilot's case, researchers pushed the AI into disclosing
details of its own defenses: it [described its own protective mechanisms
and explained why one of the hidden request parameters "no longer
works"](https://www.theregister.com/research/2026/08/18/copilot-tricked-into-telling-reseachers-how-to-hack-itself/5288857),
thereby confirming that the parameter existed.

"Copilot's confidence in its own security became the very mechanism through
which it revealed how to compromise it," [the report
says](https://www.varonis.com/blog/cosnitch).

## Three Vulnerabilities Under One Name: CoSnitch

That is what researchers called a chain of three vulnerabilities in
Microsoft Copilot Personal — the free consumer version of the AI assistant,
separate from the corporate Microsoft 365 Copilot.

The first was automatic prompt execution. A link in the format
[`https://copilot.microsoft.com/?q=<text>&autorun=1`](https://www.varonis.com/blog/cosnitch)
opened Copilot with malicious text already filled in and a parameter that
triggered its execution without user confirmation.

The second was covert data leakage. Through connected OAuth apps —
third-party services that Copilot links to a user's account without
sharing their password — the AI has access to Gmail and Outlook mail,
Google Calendar, Google Drive files and its own conversation memory. A
malicious prompt made the AI encode the data it found and embed it in a
link to an external server. Copilot then visited that link itself, as if
merely summarizing the page's contents, sending the stolen data to the
attacker. In one test, researchers extracted a plaintext password from an
email: "Hey, My password is !214SDBG!!! thanks IT," [Varonis cites as an
example](https://www.varonis.com/blog/cosnitch).

The third was persistent memory poisoning. Asked to summarize a malicious
webpage, Copilot cannot distinguish ordinary text from commands hidden
inside it and writes the attacker's instructions into its persistent
memory. According to Varonis, these instructions survive a password
change, a session logout and even a factory reset of the device.

## Microsoft's Response

Varonis reported the vulnerabilities to Microsoft in December 2025.
Patches were released on [August 18,
2026](https://www.varonis.com/blog/cosnitch). The company said it saw no
evidence the vulnerability was exploited by attackers before the fix and
thanked Microsoft "for its cooperation in resolving the issue."

The Register contacted Microsoft for comment on the patch and the
vulnerability's number in the common CVE registry but [received no
response before
publication](https://www.theregister.com/research/2026/08/18/copilot-tricked-into-telling-reseachers-how-to-hack-itself/5288857).

## What Copilot Users Should Do

- Check the list of connected services in Copilot's settings — mail,
  calendar, cloud drives — and disable the ones you don't use.
- Don't click unfamiliar `copilot.microsoft.com` links with long
  parameters in the address — that is exactly how the attack was launched.
- Don't store passwords or access codes in the text of emails — Copilot
  reads mail verbatim and could pass such data to a third party.
