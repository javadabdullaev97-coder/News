Britain and Google on August 18 announced the launch of a pilot project called Operation Blue Skies: artificial intelligence will help British air traffic controllers adjust aircraft routes and altitudes over the Atlantic to reduce their trails.

These are contrails — condensation trails, the white streaks behind aircraft that form when water vapor in engine exhaust condenses around soot particles. According to [Google](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/blue-skies/), such trails account for roughly a third of aviation's total climate impact. According to [NATS](https://nats.aero/blog/2026/08/navigating-around-climate-impact-the-contrails-opportunity/), just 2-3% of all flights produce about 80% of the global warming caused by contrails, meaning targeted measures could have a significant effect.

Besides Google, the Operation Blue Skies project includes British air navigation service provider NATS, the Met Office weather service, the Contrails.org research group, Imperial College London and the University of Cambridge. The program is designed to run for 30 months and is co-funded by Britain's Department for Transport through the ATI program, [Google said](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/blue-skies/).

Google will contribute £1.4 million to the project in the form of expertise and computing power provided free of charge, the company said in a statement. Britain's Department for Transport allocated another £2.6 million for the 30-month project, [The Register reported](https://www.theregister.com/public-sector/2026/08/18/uk-puts-google-ai-on-the-flight-path-to-fewer-contrails/5288516).

The trials will take place in the Shanwick Oceanic airspace — the eastern part of the North Atlantic air corridor controlled by NATS. According to Google's estimate, this zone accounts for about 5% of global warming caused by aircraft contrails. About 10,000 flights pass through it annually during trial hours, the company said.

Each of the two trial phases will last about four months, according to Google's statement. NATS controllers will use standard communication procedures to instruct pilots to make small changes to their route or altitude. Such maneuvers could increase fuel consumption and CO2 emissions, so the trials are meant to show whether the benefit of reduced contrails outweighs these costs, [The Register noted](https://www.theregister.com/public-sector/2026/08/18/uk-puts-google-ai-on-the-flight-path-to-fewer-contrails/5288516).

"This partnership with Google will help British experts find practical ways to make flights cleaner. Small changes to routes over the Atlantic will help reduce aircraft trails," Britain's Aviation Minister Keir Mather said, according to [The Register](https://www.theregister.com/public-sector/2026/08/18/uk-puts-google-ai-on-the-flight-path-to-fewer-contrails/5288516).

## Results With American Airlines

Google had already tested a similar approach in the United States together with American Airlines. In 2023, the companies conducted their first trial on 70 flights and cut contrail formation by 54%. This was the first proof that airlines could measurably reduce the climate impact of contrails, according to a [Google publication](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-research/contrail-avoidance-research/) dated March 19, 2026.

In the new study, Google built its AI forecasts into American Airlines' flight planning software and tested them on 2,400 transatlantic flights. For flights that successfully performed the avoidance maneuver, contrail formation dropped 62% compared with the control group, the company said.

## What's Next

Flight trials will take place in the winters of 2026/27 and 2027/28, [The Register reported](https://www.theregister.com/public-sector/2026/08/18/uk-puts-google-ai-on-the-flight-path-to-fewer-contrails/5288516). Imperial College London and the University of Cambridge will conduct an independent assessment of the climate impact. Contrails.org is responsible for the forecast models and trial parameters — all three are members of the Operation Blue Skies consortium.
