MIT and Stanford researchers showed on August 18, using data from 24,521 conversations with AI chatbots, that work-related use scenarios occur less often than in reports from Anthropic and OpenAI.

The [AI Observatory](https://www.dataprovenance.org/ai_observatory.pdf) project is run by Shane Longpre of MIT Media Lab and Anka Royel of Stanford's Trustworthy AI Research Lab. Both project leads work in university labs — MIT and Stanford — rather than at the chatbot developer companies themselves. The researchers collected 85,633 messages from 24,521 conversations — data from 5,000 users across 52 models, including ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Grok, covering 2023–2025. The material comes from seven open datasets collected with participants' consent.

The authors applied the same filtering method to the collected data that Anthropic uses to select conversations for its Economic Index report on work-related use of Claude. The result: [48% of conversations](https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/08/18/1142226/how-people-use-ai/) would not have passed such a filter — they would have been classified as non-work-related. Among the filtered-out topics, personal matters — health, relationships, everyday affairs — were more common.

## Different Chatbots, Different Habits

According to the project's data, users more often choose Claude for programming, Gemini for role-play and creative scenarios, and ChatGPT for help with studies and homework. Individual companies' reports do not show these differences: each publishes data only about its own product.

The authors call AI Observatory an open alternative to corporate reports — with data that any independent researcher can verify. So far, the project covers conversations from 5,000 people — noticeably fewer than the closed datasets Anthropic and OpenAI use for their own reports.
