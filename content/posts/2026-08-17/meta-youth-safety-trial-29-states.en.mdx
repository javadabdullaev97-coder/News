A federal court in Oakland began jury selection on August 12 in a states' lawsuit against Meta Platforms — the company is accused of fostering addiction among children on Facebook and Instagram, [the California Attorney General's Office reports](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-celebrates-yet-another-court-order-denying-meta%E2%80%99s-attempt).

The case [People of the State of California v. Meta Platforms, Inc.](https://cand.uscourts.gov/cases-e-filing/cases/423-cv-05448-ygr/people-state-california-v-meta-platforms-inc-et-al) (No. 4:23-cv-05448-YGR) is being heard by Judge Yvonne Gonzalez Rogers in the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California. The lawsuit was filed on October 24, 2023.

The plaintiffs' interests in court are represented by attorneys general from a coalition of states led by California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey. The plaintiffs allege that Meta designed the infinite feed and push notifications to keep minors in its apps as long as possible. The company is also accused of illegally collecting data from users under 13 without parental consent — a violation of the federal [COPPA](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/ahead-meta-trial-attorney-general-bonta-secures-critical-win) law, the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act.

According to estimates from several business outlets the editorial team could not access directly, the potential fine facing Meta could reach $1.4 trillion. That figure, according to the same outlets, was disclosed by the company itself in a court filing. California Attorney General Rob Bonta compared the case to lawsuits against tobacco companies in the 1990s. According to his remarks, [carried by Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/videos/2026-08-16/bonta-says-meta-put-profit-over-children-s-safety-video), Meta knew its product harmed children and publicly denied it.

## Meta's position

The company denies the allegations. Meta's response to the allegations was reported by business outlets including Bloomberg — the editorial team could not access the materials directly. According to their reporting, the company's statement says that in developing products for teens it listened to parents and worked with experts and law enforcement. The plaintiffs' $1.4 trillion estimate was called unfounded by Meta, according to the same reporting. According to the company, a penalty of this scale has no precedent in the history of consumer-protection enforcement.

Before the trial began, the company lost twice on appeal. On June 30, [the court denied](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/ahead-meta-trial-attorney-general-bonta-secures-critical-win) Meta's motion to dismiss the case. The judge found that the company had not obtained parental consent for collecting children's data in the form required by COPPA. On August 10, the Ninth Circuit Court of Appeals [denied](https://oag.ca.gov/news/press-releases/attorney-general-bonta-celebrates-yet-another-court-order-denying-meta%E2%80%99s-attempt) Meta's appeal based on Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act and did not halt the proceedings. "Meta designed a dangerous product for young users, knew about that danger, and lied to children, families and the public about how dangerous it was," Bonta said at the time.

According to the California Attorney General's Office, opening statements from both sides begin on August 18.
