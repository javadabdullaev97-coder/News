The Central Bank of the Republic of Uzbekistan (CBU) explained in early August, on its Telegram channel, how prices for imported and domestic goods shape overall inflation in the country, which accelerated to 6.4% in June.

## How the Central Bank explains the structure of inflation

Inflation is not made up solely of domestic-market prices, [the publication states](https://t.me/centralbankuzbekistan/13393): part of the price growth stems from changes in the cost of imported goods. When food, fuel, medicine, equipment, or raw materials are brought into the country, their final price is affected by world quotations and exchange-rate fluctuations, the regulator explains.

Domestic-market goods and services, according to the same explanation, drive inflation differently — through production costs, wages, transport, energy, rent, and the balance of supply and demand. That is why, when analyzing inflation, the Central Bank assesses import and domestic factors separately: import-driven inflation is more closely tied to external shocks, while domestic inflation is tied to demand, costs, and market conditions.

The post contains no numerical breakdown of what share of current inflation comes from imported goods and what share from domestic ones: the publication is explanatory rather than statistical in nature.

## Inflation is already near the upper bound of the annual forecast

The explanation came out against a backdrop of accelerating price growth. According to the Central Bank, annual inflation in June stood at [6.4%](https://cbu.uz/ru/monetary-policy/annual-inflation/indicators/), with core inflation (excluding seasonal and regulated prices) at 5.7%. The inflation forecast for the end of 2026 [was kept unchanged at 6.5%](https://cbu.uz/ru/monetary-policy/publications/press-releases/4256870/) — according to the press release following the Central Bank board's meeting on July 29, 2026. At the same meeting, the regulator kept the key rate at 14% per annum.

In the same press release, the Central Bank directly names the import channel as one of the risks to inflation through the end of the year: amid geopolitical tension, risks of rising food and raw-material prices persist, and fuel supply disruptions among trading partners, combined with higher logistics costs, could create "additional pressure on domestic inflation through import prices."

## The som exchange rate — one of the channels of import-driven inflation

One of the channels through which world prices feed into domestic inflation is the som's exchange rate against the dollar: the weaker the som, the more expensive imports become. In early August, the official dollar exchange rate [rose for a second consecutive trading day](https://leap.uz/en/2026/08/08/usd-exchange-rate-climbing) — LEAP News covered the details separately.
