The U.S. Federal Communications Commission (FCC) on July 28 added foreign-made advanced robots — humanoids, quadrupeds and autonomous mobile platforms — to its list of equipment banned from import on national security grounds.

The decision was issued as [Public Notice DA 26-786](https://docs.fcc.gov/public/attachments/DA-26-786A1.pdf) by the FCC's Public Safety and Homeland Security Bureau. Its legal basis is the Secure and Trusted Communications Networks Act of 2019. Under that law, the agency maintains a [Covered List](https://www.fcc.gov/supplychain/coveredlist) of equipment that serves the interests of foreign governments designated as threats to U.S. security.

The ban covers devices weighing more than 2 kg (4.4 lbs) that can move on their own and carry sensors and a communications module of at least 200 kilobits per second. That includes humanoid robots, robot dogs, household robotic vacuum cleaners and lawnmowers, according to [K&L Gates](https://www.klgates.com/thought-leadership/FCC-Adds-Foreign-Produced-Advanced-Robotic-Devices-to-the-Covered-List-Five-Things-to-Know-8-3-2026). Exceptions apply to cars, trains, unmanned aerial vehicles, medical devices and stationary industrial manipulators.

According to K&L Gates, the FCC justified the decision by citing the risk that autonomous, connected robots could collect sensitive data or be used for intelligence-gathering on behalf of foreign governments. The devices could also be vulnerable to remote hacking.

The ban applies only to new models: they will not be able to receive the FCC certification required to import, sell or advertise the equipment in the United States. Shipments imported and certified before July 28 can continue to be sold and operated. A manufacturer may obtain an individual waiver from the U.S. Department of War (formerly the Department of Defense) or demonstrate that American-made components account for more than 65% of the device's value. That threshold will rise to 70% by 2029. The requirement on the share of American parts takes effect 30 days after publication in the Federal Register.

## Unitree distributor shifts assembly to the U.S.

RoboStore, the U.S. distributor of Chinese humanoid robots that supplies, among others, [Unitree](https://arstechnica.com/gadgets/2026/08/us-distributor-of-chinas-most-popular-humanoid-robots-pivots-after-us-ban/) models, responded to the ban by launching a new entity called Robo Inc, according to Ars Technica. Per the outlet, Robo Inc will handle assembly, software integration and security vetting of the robots on U.S. soil — under a "global design, local assembly" model.

According to Ars Technica, the company expects to open a facility on Long Island in January 2027, where final assembly and fine-tuning of the robots is planned.
