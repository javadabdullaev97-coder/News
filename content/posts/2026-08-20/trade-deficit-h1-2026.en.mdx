Uzbekistan's foreign trade deficit rose to $9.2 billion in January-June 2026: imports increased 21%, while exports fell 8.8%, [according to data from the National Statistics Committee of the Republic of Uzbekistan](https://stat.uz/ru/press-tsentr/novosti-goskomstata/69502-zbekistonning-tash-i-savdo-ajlanmasi-yarim-jilda-41-mlrd-dollarga-etdi-4).

The country's total foreign trade turnover reached $41 billion — 7.4% more than in the first half of 2025. Exports fell to $15.9 billion, while imports rose to $25.1 billion. The gap between them — $9.2 billion — is nearly three times last year's figure.

The decline in exports is mainly due to a drop in gold shipments. Excluding gold, merchandise exports actually rose 28.7% to $8.2 billion, driven by food, chemical products and textiles.

China remained the largest trading partner, accounting for 23.1% of turnover, followed by Russia (17.1%), Kazakhstan (6.8%), Turkey (3.4%) and Afghanistan (2.6%). Uzbekistan traded with 195 countries in total.

## Why the soum's exchange rate stayed stable

Despite the growing trade deficit, the soum's exchange rate barely changed over the half-year, [according to a review by the Central Bank of the Republic of Uzbekistan](https://cbu.uz/upload/iblock/7bb/22prqsb717ddmitk1ad7ms5qmnxy15kb/Ichki-valyuta-bozori-sharhi-_-I-yarim-yillik-_2_.pdf). It stayed within a range of 11,935–12,320 soums per dollar and closed the period at 12,009 soums — unchanged from the start of the year.

In the domestic currency market, the supply of foreign currency grew faster than demand. Over the half-year, demand rose 20% to nearly $32 billion, while supply rose 31% to $27.8 billion. The gap between them narrowed to roughly $4.2 billion, down from $5.4 billion a year earlier.

The main source of additional supply was the population. The population sold banks $12.3 billion — nearly 1.4 times more than in the first half of 2025 — and bought $6.8 billion (up 26%). International money transfers provided another $8 billion in net inflow: $9.3 billion came into the country, while $1.3 billion left.

In June, the International Monetary Fund revised its assessment of Uzbekistan's exchange rate regime following Article IV consultations — the Fund's annual review of the country's economic policy. The status changed from "crawling" to "floating," the same regime used in Kazakhstan, Turkey and nearly thirty other countries.
