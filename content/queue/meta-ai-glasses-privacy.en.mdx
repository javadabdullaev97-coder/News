Sales of Meta's camera- and AI-equipped glasses more than tripled in 2025 — business media, citing Italy's EssilorLuxottica, maker of the Ray-Ban Meta line, [reported](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/11/ray-ban-maker-essilorluxottica-triples-sales-of-meta-ai-glasses.html) in February. As demand grows, so does the number of apps promising to warn users about nearby wearers of such glasses — though their developers say themselves that they offer no guarantee.

Meta's glasses combine a camera, microphone and an AI voice assistant in a frame that looks no different from ordinary Ray-Ban or Oakley sunglasses. The more such glasses are sold, the higher the chance of meeting an owner on the street or in a café — and there's no way to tell by looking whether recording is underway.

## Demand outpaces production

According to business media, including [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/02/11/ray-ban-maker-essilorluxottica-triples-sales-of-meta-ai-glasses.html), sales of Meta's AI glasses more than tripled by the end of 2025. They topped 7 million units, compared with roughly 2 million combined in 2023–2024. Direct access to EssilorLuxottica's press release was not obtained by publication time.

A separate model with a display in the lens, Ray-Ban Display, faced an even more acute shortage. As Meta's blog reported in January (citing a [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/meta-ai-glasses-see-unprecedented-132100914.html) report), the product launched with "extremely limited stock." "Unprecedented interest" forced the company to push back its international launch — the waitlist now stretches through all of 2026.

## How detector apps work

The best-known of these apps is Zuckoff. [It listens to Bluetooth traffic](https://zuckoff.pawels-apps.workers.dev/) and identifies the model — Ray-Ban Meta, Oakley Meta or Snap Spectacles — from the service codes a device broadcasts when connecting. Developer Pawel Shidlovski warns plainly on the app's website: silence doesn't prove no one is recording, and a detector alert doesn't prove someone is.

Glasses most often give themselves away with a signal when powered on, connected to a phone, or removed from their case. After that, they may "go quiet" for a third-party scanner.

A rival app, Antizuck, [climbed to third place](https://9to5mac.com/2026/07/31/antizuck-ios-app-that-claims-to-detect-nearby-smart-glasses-tops-app-store-chart/) in the US App Store's paid section on July 31, priced at $3. 9to5Mac did not independently verify the app and said so plainly, finding neither a description of its detection technology nor an official response from the developer.

## What Meta says

Meta's official support page [lists the data](https://www.meta.com/help/ai-glasses/483508126732797/) the glasses collect: Bluetooth connection status, battery level, Wi-Fi status and how often the device is used. Owners can turn off transmission of part of this statistics to the company. The page notes separately that the choice to share additional data doesn't apply to photos and video captured by the glasses — those remain outside that setting.

The page says nothing about the privacy of people near the glasses' owner. There is no recording indicator light, and no instructions for a bystander on how to tell they're being filmed.

## What to do if someone nearby is wearing such glasses

- Detector apps like Zuckoff and Antizuck register only a device's Bluetooth proximity, not the fact of recording itself — they don't work as proof.
- Antizuck hasn't been independently verified yet: 9to5Mac hasn't confirmed the app actually detects the glasses.
- Meta's support page explains how to turn off data transmission about your own glasses, but offers no tools to someone who might be filmed without their knowledge.
- The only reliable way to know whether recording is happening is to ask the glasses' owner directly.
